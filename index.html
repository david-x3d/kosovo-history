Kosovo

Kosova · Косово

Eight thousand years on a small high plain in the Balkans, told in order, with both sides of the story.

The name

A field of blackbirds

The plain at dusk1/8

Kosovo takes its name from kos, the Serbian word for blackbird. Kosovo Polje, the plain west of Pristina where a famous battle was fought in 1389, means the field of blackbirds. In Albanian the country is Kosova or Kosovë.

It covers 10,887 square kilometres, about the size of Jamaica, and around 1.6 million people live there. Few places that small carry as much history, or as much disagreement about it. Albanians and Serbs often remember the same events in different ways. This page goes in order, sticks to what historians broadly agree on, and says where they don't.

c. 6000 – 3000 BC

Clay goddesses

The first farmers settled the river valleys around eight thousand years ago. They grew wheat and barley, kept sheep and goats, and lived in houses of wattle and daub. Their villages belonged to the Starčevo and later the Vinča cultures, which spread across the central Balkans.

In the 1950s, digging at Tjerrtorja in Pristina turned up a small terracotta figure of a seated woman with a five-sided, mask-like face. Archaeologists call her the Goddess on the Throne. She has become a symbol of the city.

c. 400 BC – AD 600

Dardania and Rome

Greek and Roman writers called the people here the Dardani. Historians still argue whether they were Illyrian, Thracian, or something of both. By the 3rd century BC they had a kingdom strong enough to raid Macedon.

Rome absorbed Dardania around the turn of the millennium. Silver and lead from the mines paid for roads and baths. The city of Ulpiana was founded under Emperor Trajan in the early 2nd century AD. After an earthquake in 518, Justinian rebuilt it and renamed it Justiniana Secunda.

Albanian historians often trace Albanian ancestry to the Dardani. Serbian historians stress the Slavic settlement that came later. The evidence for either kind of continuity is thin.

1180s – 1455

Domes, frescoes, silver

Slavs settled the Balkans in the 6th and 7th centuries. The region passed between Byzantium and Bulgaria until the Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja took it at the end of the 12th century. For the next two hundred years it was the heart of the Serbian state.

The Serbian church moved its seat to Peja, which became a patriarchate in 1346. Kings paid for monasteries: Gračanica in 1321, Visoki Dečani between 1327 and 1335. Their frescoes, thousands of figures in lapis blue and gold, are on UNESCO's World Heritage list, and on its list of sites in danger since 2006.

The mines at Novo Brdo were among the biggest silver producers in Europe. The Dečani charters of 1330 list the villages that worked for the monastery, including Albanian ones: an early written trace of Albanians in the area.

28 June 1389

The battle on the field of blackbirds

On St Vitus' Day, a Christian coalition under Prince Lazar of Serbia met the army of the Ottoman sultan Murad I on the plain near Pristina. Lazar's side included Bosnian troops and, by most accounts, Albanian lords. Both Lazar and Murad died. Tradition says a knight named Miloš Obilić got into the sultan's tent and stabbed him.

The battle itself was probably a draw. Both armies were wrecked, but the Ottomans could replace their losses and Serbia could not. Within a few years Lazar's son was an Ottoman vassal.

What the battle became matters more than what it was. Serbian epic songs turned Lazar into a martyr who chose a heavenly kingdom over an earthly one, and the field into sacred ground. Legend says the red peonies that grow there came from the blood of the fallen. Murad's tomb still stands near the battlefield.

1455 – 1912

Four and a half centuries

Novo Brdo fell in 1455, and the region stayed in the Ottoman Empire for more than 450 years. Prizren grew into a trading town of stone bridges, bathhouses and mosques; the Sinan Pasha Mosque went up in 1615. Over these centuries many Albanians converted to Islam, while most Serbs stayed Orthodox.

In 1690, after a failed Habsburg campaign, Patriarch Arsenije III led a large number of Serbs north across the Danube. Serbian tradition calls it the Great Migration. Historians argue about its size, but over the following centuries the population tilted towards an Albanian majority.

In June 1878, Albanian leaders met in Prizren to oppose plans at the Congress of Berlin to hand Albanian-inhabited land to Serbia, Montenegro and Greece. The League of Prizren is counted as the start of the Albanian national movement.

1912 – 1945

Wars and new borders

In 1912 the Ottomans were gone within weeks. Serbs saw a homecoming. Albanians, by then the majority, saw a conquest.

In the First Balkan War, Serbian and Montenegrin armies took Kosovo. An international inquiry sent by the Carnegie Endowment documented massacres and burned villages. In the winter of 1915 the Serbian army retreated the other way, through Kosovo and over the Albanian mountains, with huge losses.

Between the wars, the Kingdom of Yugoslavia settled Serb and Montenegrin colonists on land taken from Albanians and pushed Albanians to emigrate to Turkey. In the Second World War most of Kosovo was joined to Italian-run Albania, and the violence ran the other way. By 1945 Kosovo was back in Yugoslavia.

1945 – 1989

Province, university, protest

Tito's Yugoslavia made Kosovo an autonomous part of Serbia. Under the security chief Aleksandar Ranković, Albanians lived under heavy police pressure until he fell in 1966. After student protests in 1968 things loosened: the University of Pristina opened in 1970, and Albanian-language schools, press and television grew.

The 1974 constitution gave Kosovo nearly the powers of a republic, with its own seat in the federal presidency. In 1981, a year after Tito's death, students protested about canteen food, then for full republic status. The army put the protests down.

In 1982 Pristina got its National Library: 99 domes under a metal net, designed by the Croatian architect Andrija Mutnjaković. People love it or hate it. Few ignore it.

Many Serbs left Kosovo in these decades, citing pressure and harassment. In Belgrade, that grievance fed a new nationalism.

1989 – 1999

Parallel lives, then war

In March 1989, Serbia under Slobodan Milošević stripped Kosovo of its autonomy. In February, miners at Trepča had gone on hunger strike underground to try to stop it. On 28 June, the 600th anniversary of the battle, Milošević spoke to a vast crowd at Gazimestan, on the old battlefield.

Through the 1990s Albanians were dismissed from state jobs, hospitals and schools. Led by the writer Ibrahim Rugova, they answered with peaceful resistance: a parallel state, with classes held in private houses and taxes paid to their own government.

By 1998 an armed group, the Kosovo Liberation Army, was fighting the Serbian police and army. Serbian offensives in Drenica killed many civilians. After 45 Albanians were killed at Račak in January 1999 and talks at Rambouillet failed, NATO bombed Yugoslavia for 78 days, from 24 March to 10 June 1999.

During the bombing, Serbian forces drove hundreds of thousands of Albanians out of the country. When NATO troops arrived in June, most came back, and many Serbs, Roma and others fled revenge attacks.

1999 – 2008

Nine years in between

UN Security Council Resolution 1244 put Kosovo under a UN mission, UNMIK, while NATO-led KFOR troops kept the peace. Kosovo stayed formally part of Serbia but was run separately.

Serbs gathered in enclaves and in the north around Mitrovica, where the bridge over the Ibar became a dividing line. In March 2004, riots across Kosovo left 19 people dead and damaged or destroyed hundreds of Serbian homes and dozens of churches and monasteries.

In 2007, UN talks led by the former Finnish president Martti Ahtisaari proposed independence under international supervision. Serbia rejected the plan, and Russia blocked it at the Security Council.

17 February 2008

Newborn

Kosovo's assembly declared independence on a Sunday afternoon. In Pristina that day, a monument of seven yellow letters was unveiled, spelling NEWBORN. Thousands of people signed it. It has been repainted on the anniversary ever since.

Serbia does not recognise the declaration and still treats Kosovo as its southern province. In 2010 the International Court of Justice gave an advisory opinion that the declaration did not violate international law. Roughly half of the UN's members recognise Kosovo; the exact count is disputed.

2008 – today

Young country, old questions

Kosovo has one of the youngest populations in Europe and a large diaspora, mostly in Germany and Switzerland. The 2024 census counted about 1.6 million residents.

The 2013 Brussels Agreement started an EU-led dialogue between Pristina and Belgrade on normalising relations. Progress has been slow and the Serb-majority north remains tense. Since January 2024, Kosovo's citizens can travel to the Schengen area without a visa.

In 2016 the judoka Majlinda Kelmendi won Kosovo's first Olympic medal, a gold, in Rio. In Dečani, monks still hold services in the 14th-century church, with NATO soldiers at the gate. Both histories are still being lived, in the same small place.
